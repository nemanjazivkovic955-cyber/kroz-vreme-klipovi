# The ENTIRE Life of Marcus Aurelius — scenario (dugi video #2, ~10 min)

Glas: George (bm_george), miran, dubok. Tekst naracije je na engleskom (publika SAD/UK).
[SCENA] = uputstvo za animaciju (lik Stoik = Marko Aurelije; ista paleta: tamna noć + topli akcenti).
Činjenice su proverene prema antičkim izvorima (Kasije Dion, Historia Augusta) i označene "ancient sources say" gde je predanje nesigurno.

---

## 0:00 — HOOK

[SCENA: noć, vojnički logor u snegu, jedan šator svetli. Kamera polako ulazi kroz otvor šatora: čovek u ogrtaču piše pri lampi.]

Two thousand years ago, the most powerful man on Earth sat alone in a freezing tent, and wrote a diary.
Not to be famous. Not for history. He wrote it only for himself.
He ruled an empire of seventy million people. He fought a plague, a war that never ended, and a friend who betrayed him.
And every night, he wrote down one thing: how to stay a good man, when everything around you falls apart.
This is the entire life of Marcus Aurelius.

## 0:40 — POGLAVLJE 1: The boy who told the truth

[SCENA: Rim, sunčano jutro, uske ulice. Mali dečak trči pored dede u togi.]

He was born in Rome in the year one hundred and twenty one.
His father died when he was about three years old, so he was raised by his mother and his grandfather.
He was a quiet, serious boy. While other rich children chased luxury, he chose to sleep on a simple mat, and he asked for less, not more.
And he had a strange habit for someone so young. He always told the truth.
The emperor Hadrian noticed him. He gave the boy a nickname: Verissimus. The truest one.

## 1:35 — POGLAVLJE 2: The heir who didn't want the throne

[SCENA: palata, mermerni stubovi. Stari car Hadrijan pokazuje na mladića. Mladić gleda u pod.]

Hadrian was old, sick, and had no son. So he made a plan.
He adopted a calm, honest senator named Antoninus, on one condition: Antoninus had to adopt young Marcus, and prepare him to rule.
Marcus was only seventeen. The ancient sources say that when he heard the news, he was not happy. He was afraid.
He knew what power does to people. He had seen it.
So instead of celebrating, he moved into the palace and began to study. Law. Speech. And then, the thing that changed his life: philosophy.
A teacher named Rusticus handed him the lessons of Epictetus, a former slave who taught that we control only one thing: how we respond.
Marcus read them again and again. The future emperor was learning from a slave.
Years later, he began his journal with a strange list. Not his victories. A list of thank-yous.
From his grandfather, he wrote, he learned good manners and a calm temper. From his mother, to be generous, and to live simply, far from the habits of the rich. From his teachers, to read carefully, and not to be satisfied with a quick, shallow understanding.
The most powerful man in the world started his private diary by thanking the people who made him.

## 2:50 — POGLAVLJE 3: Emperor at forty, and a plague

[SCENA: krunisanje, ali dva prestola. Zatim mapa carstva koja tamni, tačke bolesti se šire.]

For twenty three years, Marcus waited and worked beside Antoninus.
Antoninus was not a conqueror. He was patient, fair, and he worked from morning to night without complaining.
Marcus watched him closely. He saw how Antoninus listened before he spoke, how he never rushed a decision, and how he treated a poor man and a senator with the same respect.
He later wrote that he wanted to be, in everything, a student of Antoninus.
When Antoninus died in the year one sixty one, the Senate offered Marcus the throne.
And he did something no Roman had ever done. He refused to rule alone. He made his adoptive brother, Lucius, emperor beside him.
Two emperors, for the first time in Roman history.
But the good times ended fast. Soldiers returning from a war in the East brought something back with them. A plague.
It spread through cities, through the army, through Rome itself. Historians believe it killed millions of people.
Marcus could not stop it. No one could. All he could control was how he led through it.

## 4:00 — POGLAVLJE 4: The emperor who sold his palace

[SCENA: forum pun ljudi. Stolovi sa zlatnim peharima, svilenim haljinama, statuama. Car stoji sa strane.]

Then came war on the northern border. Germanic tribes crossed the Danube and pushed into the empire.
Rome needed soldiers. Soldiers needed money. And the treasury was empty.
Any other emperor would have raised taxes on his people.
Marcus did something else. The ancient sources say he took the treasures of the palace, gold cups, statues, paintings, even his wife's silk dresses, and sold them at a public auction in the middle of Rome.
For two months, ordinary Romans could buy the emperor's furniture.
He did not need those things. The empire needed what they were worth.

## 5:00 — POGLAVLJE 5: The war that never ended

[SCENA: zima na Dunavu, reka zaleđena, logor. Car sa vojnicima kraj vatre. Zatim šator, lampa, pisanje.]

Marcus went north himself. He was not a young soldier. He was in his late forties, often sick, with a weak stomach and little sleep.
He spent most of his last twelve years in army camps along the Danube, in the cold, far from Rome.
Some of those camps stood near cities that still exist today: Vienna, and Sirmium, now Sremska Mitrovica in Serbia.
And it was there, at night, after the meetings and the funerals, that he wrote his journal.
He called it simply: To Himself. Today we call it Meditations.
It is not a book of victories. It is a man arguing with himself.
Don't complain. Do your duty. Be kind to people who are not kind to you. Remember that every pain passes.
He wrote it the way you would write a note on your bathroom mirror. Because he needed the reminder, every single day.

## 6:30 — POGLAVLJE 6: The friend who betrayed him

[SCENA: istočna provincija, general podiže mač pred vojskom. Zatim car čita pismo, lice mirno.]

In the year one seventy five, a false rumor spread that Marcus had died.
His most trusted general in the East, Avidius Cassius, believed it, or wanted to believe it, and declared himself emperor.
Half the empire waited to see what Marcus would do.
He did not curse him. He told his soldiers he only hoped to forgive Cassius, face to face.
He never got the chance. Within three months, Cassius was killed by his own men.
And then, the ancient sources say, Marcus was handed the traitor's letters, the names of everyone who had secretly supported him.
He burned them. Without reading a single one.
He did not want to know who his enemies were. He wanted to keep living without hatred.

## 7:35 — POGLAVLJE 7: The setting sun

[SCENA: zalazak sunca nad logorom. Stari car leži, mladi oficir na vratima šatora. Svetlo polako gasne.]

The same year, his wife Faustina died. He had already buried many of his children. Most of them did not live to be adults.
He kept going. Back to the frontier. Back to the work.
In March of the year one eighty, he fell ill in a camp on the Danube. He was fifty eight.
The ancient sources say that when an officer came to ask him for the day's password, the dying emperor said:
Go to the rising sun. I am already setting.
He stopped eating, and a few days later, he was gone.
Rome had lost the last of what historians would later call the Five Good Emperors.

## 8:25 — POGLAVLJE 8: What he left us

[SCENA: moderni grad noću, mladić na krevetu sa telefonom, zatim ista knjiga Meditations u njegovim rukama, svetlo lampe kao u šatoru.]

He never meant for anyone to read his journal. But someone saved it. And two thousand years later, it is still sold in every bookstore in the world.
Because the problems he wrote about are our problems.
Stress. Angry people. Loss. Fear of the future.
And his answers still work.
One. You cannot control what happens. Only how you respond.
Two. Don't waste your energy on people who wrong you. Do your work.
Three. Every hard day ends. Every one.
Four. Be grateful for the people who shaped you. Nobody becomes strong alone.
Five. Live as if your time is limited. Because it is.
He had more power than any man alive. And he chose to spend it becoming a better person.
